High-level design · proof of concept

Product Locator HLD

In-store product search, availability, location and optimal routing across a three-floor store, orchestrated with LangGraph and served from a single Streamlit page.

Requirements in the specification, not here Scope one store, synthetic data Stack LangGraph · Python · Streamlit, in-process

1  What this document owns

The requirements live in the specification — 24 functional, 11 non-functional and 17 acceptance criteria, every one confirmed. They are not repeated here. This document owns the component architecture, the search design, the interface and the edge cases, and it is written to be read after the specification.

Three constraints shape everything below: LangGraph orchestrates the pipeline (C-01), the implementation is Python (C-02), and the interface is a single Streamlit page calling the compiled graph in-process (C-03). There is no HTTP layer anywhere in this design.

1.1  What the harder requirements mean for the design

Multiple floors — FR-10, FR-14 and A-03

No single requirement says "the store has floors": A-03 fixes the demo store at three, FR-10 makes floor part of every location, and FR-14 lets a route cross them. Together they change four things:

  • The address. Floor is part of a location, not metadata about it. "Aisle 7" is meaningless alone — there is an Aisle 7 on every floor.
  • The graph is not a flat plane. Three floor plates, and no edge joins one to another except through a vertical connector. You cannot get between floors any other way. This is precisely why A* is awkward here: straight-line distance assumes you can walk through a ceiling.
  • Routing changes. A floor transition costs more than its metres — you wait for a lift, then ride it — so connectors are weighted edges with a penalty, and a multi-product route has to group stops by floor rather than treating every stop alike.
  • The UI needs one map per floor with a switcher, and the route drawn across plates with transition markers.

For the proof of concept: three floors, one zone per floor, two vertical cores (front and rear).

FR-10 · Racks are addressable

"Addressable" means the rack has its own identifier that the system can store, look up and route to. It is a real entity in the data model with its own node in the graph — not a rectangle on a drawing.

Not addressableAddressable

  • Honest distances. If only aisles were nodes, every product in Aisle 7 would sit at the same distance. Rack nodes make the metres real.
  • A stable key. F1-A07-R03-S02 identifies a physical slot; the planogram is a mapping of SKU onto that address. A range change rewrites the mapping, not the map.
  • Shelf position becomes sayable — eye level, bottom shelf — which is what spoken directions need.

FR-02, FR-21 and FR-23 · three different things

These are routinely confused, and the distinction is where a review will probe:

  • FR-02 — one search returns many products.
  • FR-21 and FR-22 — results are grouped by availability, and one action moves only the available ones into the basket.
  • FR-23 — many searches contribute to one basket, and the basket is what gets routed.

FR-02 and FR-23 are orthogonal; both feed the same basket. The basket is not the mechanism for multi-item search, and saying it is will not survive a follow-up question.

FR-05 · Ambiguous search asks rather than guessing

"bread" matches forty products with similar scores. Rather than picking one, the graph pauses on a real interrupt() and asks, showing the top options. State is preserved, so in a batch query only the ambiguous term asks — the others stay resolved. Capped at two rounds, after which it shows the ranked list and stops asking.

Confirmed, and AC-03 tests the part that matters: the chosen option must resolve without the shopper re-entering the other terms. A clarification that loses the rest of the search is worse than a ranked list.

FR-09 · Out-of-stock items offer alternatives

Eggs are out, so instead of a dead end the shopper sees two or three in-stock alternatives from the same category. The mechanic that matters: candidates are filtered on structured dietary attributes before the model sees them, so it chooses among already-safe options rather than judging safety. Nothing generated asserts that a product is gluten free, vegan or nut free — that claim comes from a catalogue attribute, passed through verbatim.

Confirmed, and AC-17 is deliberately strict: every candidate offered must itself be in stock and compatible with the original's recorded dietary attributes, and where no compatible alternative exists the system says so rather than offering an incompatible one. A substitute is the one place a shopper could be handed something their diet excludes, which is why the filter runs before the model rather than after it.

2  System layers

Five layers. Requests move down; facts move back up. Nothing above the services layer reads a file, and nothing below the orchestration layer talks to a model.

2.1  Agent decomposition

Eleven nodes on explicit edges, grouped by the job they do. Read as agents, they are six.

RoleNodesModel
Query understandingclassify_query · understand_query · resolve_items · clarifyunderstand_query only — it produces a typed filter, never a product name
Availabilitycheck_availability · suggest_substitutessuggest_substitutes only, over a candidate set already filtered for stock and diet
Locationresolve_locationsNone
Routingroute_single · route_multiNone
Narrationdescribe_routePhrasing only, over facts it is handed
VerificationverifyNone — it is the check on everything above it

Three nodes call a model; eight are plain functions. Every node that decides where a shopper walks — location and both routing nodes — sits in the model-free half, which is NFR-01 expressed as a graph rather than as a promise. Each model node has a named deterministic fallback (EC-37), so NFR-04 holds and AC-11 can prove it.

Why explicit edges rather than a tool-calling agent. The sequence is known: availability is always checked before a route is planned, because a route to an empty shelf is worse than no route at all. An agent that re-decides the order on every query can get that wrong, and buys nothing in exchange. The fixed order is a safety property, not a missing feature.

What makes this agentic is the rest of the machinery: shared typed state, a checkpointer, the clarify cycle with its own termination counter, and a real interrupt() that suspends a journey mid-flight and resumes it with the already resolved terms intact. Those are the parts a plain script cannot do.

verify — the last gate

The final node before the page. It takes the composed answer and re-checks every factual claim in it against the service results that produced them: each SKU, floor, aisle, rack and shelf, each availability statement, the route distance, and the floor transitions named in the directions.

A claim that does not match is not corrected and shown — it is never shown. The node discards the composed text and re-renders from the deterministic template, exactly as it would if the model had been unavailable, and records the discrepancy in degraded.

Two reasons it is a node and not a helper function. It must be impossible to reach the page without passing through it, which an edge guarantees and a convention does not. And it is the component that makes NFR-02 something the system does rather than a property it is claimed to have — AC-10 feeds in a deliberately corrupted claim and asserts it never reaches the shopper.

Grounding is enforced twice over, and the two are not redundant: the types stop the model naming a product or a location at all, and verify catches everything downstream of that — a stale cached figure, a mis-joined placement, a template that formatted the wrong leg. One prevents invention, the other catches error.

2.2  How one request flows

A shopper standing in the Bakery on Floor 1 types milk, bread, crayons, batteries. The layer stack says what exists; this says what happens.

First graph run · search
  1. The page collects the query, the start point and the step-free switch, and calls graph.invoke with a new thread_id.
  2. classify_query splits on the commas — a batch of four terms. No model involved; it is a regex over delimiters and word count.
  3. resolve_items asks the catalogue for ranked candidates per term.
  4. "bread" comes back with twelve near-equal hits, so the graph raises a real interrupt() and stops. The other three terms stay resolved in state.
Pause · the shopper answers
  1. The page shows the top options. The shopper taps sourdough.
  2. The next page run resumes the same thread_id with that choice. Nothing is re-searched and nothing is re-typed.
  3. check_availability takes all four SKUs in one call and returns a map: three in stock, crayons out.
  4. suggest_substitutes offers in-stock alternatives for the crayons, chosen from candidates already filtered on stock and dietary attributes.
  5. resolve_locations returns every placement; routing will use the nearest.
  6. Results are grouped, available first, with one action that moves only those three into the basket.
Second graph run · routing
  1. The shopper taps plan route. The basket — not the search result — is the input.
  2. route_multi clusters the stops by floor, takes the highest floor first, and runs open-path 2-opt within each floor leg. Distances are matrix lookups, precomputed at startup.
  3. describe_route phrases the directions from the facts it is handed.
  4. verify re-checks every claim against the service results. Only then does the page render.

Three things in that sequence are the design, not decoration. The interrupt at step 4 keeps the other three terms resolved, so a clarification costs one tap and not a retyped search. The basket gate at step 10 is why no route is ever planned to the empty crayon shelf. And step 14 stands between every answer and the shopper.

3  Search design

This section exists because it is what got asked and what had no answer. Three separate questions hide inside "how does search work": what matching mechanism, can several products go in at once, and can their availability be checked in a single pass. They have three different answers.

3.1  The matching mechanism

Five stages, tried in order, cheapest first. A query stops at the first stage that produces confident results.

StageMechanismHandlesImplementation
1 · ExactDictionary lookup on the normalised name and known synonyms"semi skimmed milk", "kitchen roll" typed as the catalogue spells themPython dict, O(1)
2 · LexicalBM25 over a normalised text field: name + brand + size + category + synonyms"coke 500ml", "hovis wholemeal" — the overwhelming majority of real queriesrank_bm25 in memory for ~200 SKUs; OpenSearch BM25 at real scale
3 · FuzzyTrigram / Levenshtein similarity, applied only when BM25 scores come back weak"paracetomol", "yoghurt" vs "yogurt"rapidfuzz, threshold-gated
4 · Structured filterModel maps natural language to typed filters, which constrain stage 2"something gluten free for lunchboxes"One structured-output call; the model names no product
5 · RankingRetail signals re-rank the candidate setPutting the right Coke firstDeterministic score function

Why BM25 and not vector search

BM25 is a ranked lexical match — it weights rare terms highly, dampens repeated ones, and normalises for field length, which is exactly right for short product names. It is also explainable: you can say why a result ranked where it did, which matters when a buyer asks why their product is not showing.

Vector search is the usual next suggestion, and it is deliberately not in the design. At 200 SKUs the semantic cases are handled better by stage 4 — the model maps intent to a category and dietary filter, and BM25 searches within it. That is cheaper, explainable, and cannot return a product that does not exist. Vector search earns its place at a 50,000-SKU catalogue where an LLM call per query is too slow; at that point the pattern is hybrid BM25 + embeddings fused with reciprocal rank fusion. Worth naming as the scale-up path, not building now.

Ranking signals, in priority order

  1. Ranged in this store — a product this branch does not carry never outranks one it does
  2. In stock — available beats unavailable
  3. BM25 relevance
  4. Exact size or variant match when the query names one ("500ml")
  5. Own-brand and popularity as a tiebreak

3.2  Several products in one search

Yes — and not via the basket. The basket accumulates items across separate searches; it is not the mechanism for putting several products into one search. That is a batch query, and it is handled before any searching happens, by classify_query.

What the three input shapes look like

InputClassified asBehaviour
coke 500mlsingle termOne product, availability, location, route

3.3  Checking availability for all of them at once

This is the part the basket answer misses, and it is a separate design point from batch search. Resolving several terms gives several SKUs; the availability check on them must be one call, not N.

check_availability takes a list of SKUs and returns a map. At POC scale that is a dict comprehension; against a real stock service it is the difference between one request and sixty. Designing the signature as a batch from the start costs nothing now and avoids an N+1 rewrite later. The same applies to resolve_locations.

So the answer to "can I check several products' availability up front" is yes, in a single pass, and the route is then offered rather than forced — the shopper sees what is in stock and where before deciding whether to walk it.

3.4  Batch results and the basket gate

Resolving several terms produces a mixed result — some in stock, some not. Routing straight off that set is wrong, because it would plan a walk to shelves that have nothing on them. So a basket gate sits between search and routing: results are grouped with the available items first, and one action moves only those into the basket.

The basket is then the input to routing, which means a route is never computed for something that is not there. It also disposes of the all-out-of-stock case without special handling — the action is simply disabled.

4  Interface and presentation

One Streamlit page, used by a shopper standing in the store on their own phone (A-06). The page is not a thin shell over the graph — four requirements land here and nowhere else.

RequirementWhat the page does
FR-15

FR-20
route shown twice		Step-by-step directions as text, and the same route drawn over one SVG floor plate per floor, with transition markers where it changes floor
NFR-10
screen reader		The directions list is the accessible route, not a caption on a drawing: ordered text, one instruction per step, naming floor, aisle, rack and shelf position. The map is never the only way to read the route
NFR-11
phone width		Single column at 390 px, the floor plate scaled to width with no pinch-zoom, controls within thumb reach, no horizontal page scroll

Explainability is a presentation requirement too (NFR-09). The page can always answer two questions, and both answers come from the mechanism itself.

Why is this product third? Because it is out of stock, and in-stock items rank above out-of-stock ones. Ranking is a list of rules in priority order, so the reason is whichever rule pushed it down.

Why did the route go this way? Entrance → Aisle 1 → Aisle 3 → checkout. The route is an ordered list of points, so printing that list is the explanation.

A model could be asked either question and would answer fluently. But it never saw the ranking rules or the path — it would be inventing a reason that sounds right, not reporting the real one. An explanation nobody can check is worse than none, because it gets believed.

4.1  Streamlit mechanics the design depends on

Streamlit re-executes the whole script on every interaction. Three consequences are design-level rather than implementation detail.

  1. The expensive objects are built once. Store graph, BM25 index and the distance and predecessor matrices live in @st.cache_resource. Without that every click rebuilds them and NFR-05's five seconds are spent before the search starts.
  2. The search box commits on submit, inside st.form, so the agent runs once per search rather than once per keystroke.
  3. The clarification pause is served by the next page run. FR-05's interrupt() suspends the graph; the shopper's answer resumes the same thread_id on the following run. No second request exists, because the page calls the graph in-process.

7  Likely review questions

What search mechanism is it?
BM25 lexical ranking over a normalised product text field, with an exact name lookup in front of it and fuzzy matching behind it for typos. Natural-language queries go through the model first, which produces a typed filter that constrains the same BM25 search — so the model narrows the search, it never names the product.
Why not vector search or embeddings?
At this catalogue size the model's structured filter handles the semantic cases more cheaply and more explainably, and it cannot return a product that does not exist. Hybrid BM25 plus embeddings fused with reciprocal rank fusion is the right answer at tens of thousands of SKUs, where an LLM call per query is too slow. It is the scale-up path, not the starting point.
If I type several products into the search bar, does it work?
Yes. classify_query detects the delimiters and splits the input, then each term resolves independently and concurrently. The basket is for accumulating items across separate searches — it is not the mechanism for multi-item search, and it should not be the answer to this question.
Can I see availability for all of them up front, before committing to a route?
Yes, in one batched call rather than one per item. The route is then offered, not forced — the shopper sees what is in stock and where it is before deciding whether to walk it.
What happens when the search is ambiguous?
The graph pauses on a real interrupt and asks, keeping the terms already resolved. Capped at two rounds, after which it shows the ranked list rather than asking again.
What algorithm computes the route?
Dijkstra, run from every node once at startup, giving a distance matrix and a predecessor matrix. Every single-product query is then an O(1) lookup. A* is the wrong tool here: it optimises one query at a time, and across floors straight-line distance is not an admissible heuristic, because a route cannot pass through a ceiling.
Do we have multiple agents?
Yes — five functional roles across eleven nodes, plus the verifier (§2.1): query understanding, availability, location, routing, narration and verification. They are LangGraph nodes on explicit edges rather than five processes negotiating with each other, which is a decision about control flow, not a shortfall in decomposition.
Is this really agentic, if the model does not choose the tools?
Yes. The agentic parts that earn their place are the shared typed state, the checkpointer that survives a page re-run, the clarify cycle with its own termination counter, and a real interrupt() that pauses a journey and resumes it with the resolved terms intact. What is deliberately not agentic is tool selection: the order is known, and letting a model re-decide it per query risks skipping the stock check and routing a shopper to an empty shelf. More autonomy over the plan, never over the facts.
How do you know the answer shown is right?
Every claim is re-checked against the service results by the verify node before the page sees it. A claim that does not match is discarded rather than corrected, and the deterministic rendering is shown instead. That is NFR-02, and AC-10 proves it by feeding in a deliberately corrupted claim.
What if the model is wrong or unavailable?
Every model node has a deterministic fallback, and no model is on the path that returns a location, a distance or a stock figure. The system degrades to plain keyword search — less fluent, never wrong about where something is.
Is this ready for a real store?
No, and it is not meant to be. The journey, the interface and the data model are proven on synthetic data. What stands between this and a real store is data, not software: a planogram feed with an owner and a contract, a stock feed with known freshness, and a machine-readable floor plan per store. The first is the long pole and it is a conversation with space planning, not an engineering task.
Is this a machine learning problem?
No. It is search and graph routing over enumerable data. BM25 is statistical ranking, not learned; Dijkstra and the 2-opt heuristic are classical algorithms with exact or provable answers. The only learned component is a pre-trained model called for language understanding — inference, not training. There is no dataset, no labels, no model to fit and no training pipeline.
Shouldn't we train a model on the store data so it learns the map?

No, and this is the most common misconception about this kind of system. The store map is not a pattern to learn, it is a fact to look up. Training is for rules you cannot enumerate; this map is 314 nodes and about 700 directed edges in six CSV files. Training a model to hold it is like training one to remember a phone number instead of writing it down.

The failure mode is the real objection: a model that has "learned" the map will confidently invent an aisle number for a product it did not memorise — exactly what this architecture exists to prevent.

What the model is for is language, not geography. It turns "something gluten free for the kids' lunchboxes" into a structured query and hands that to BM25, which searches the real catalogue. It never knows where anything is, and its output schema carries no field for a product name or a location, so it cannot.

Google Maps does not train a model to learn the road network — it stores roads as a graph and runs a routing algorithm. Its machine learning is for traffic prediction and for understanding "coffee near me", not for knowing where streets are. The same split applies here.

Training would be the right call with click logs and a learning-to-rank goal, or shelf photos and a compliance-detection goal. Neither is this project.

8  Onboarding a new store

FR-25 says a different store is four replaced files and no code changes. This is what that costs in practice, and it is the honest answer to "could we run this in a real branch?"

What a store suppliesWhat has to happen to it
Planogram as a spreadsheetRename and normalise columns, fix bay and shelf numbering, join product details, and attach every placement to a map point. Scripted, roughly a day for a first store
Planogram as a PDF or imagesTable extraction, then a person checks every row. Not attempted here, and the reason this is a data problem rather than a software one
Floor plan as an image or CADSomeone marks the walkable points and the paths between them, once per floor, and sets the scale. CAD gives exact coordinates; an image needs tracing
StockSynthetic here. A live feed replaces one service, because nothing above the service layer knows where stock comes from

The link people forget

Every rack in the planogram must name a point on the map. Without it the system can tell a shopper the aisle but cannot route them to it — the planogram and the map are two separate things, and the join between them is what makes a location walkable.

The running system never reads a PDF or an image. Conversion happens once, before the store folder exists, and the validator refuses the folder if the result is inconsistent (NFR-07).

9  Where this design is weakest

Say these before someone finds them.

A planogram says where a product is supposed to be. Shelves drift from plan within days of a range change. Synthetic data hides this completely, so the demo will look more reliable than a real deployment would. The honest mitigations are a shopper "this is wrong" report and, longer term, vision-based shelf compliance.

The start point is self-reported. FR-15 has the shopper pick a floor and an aisle, because live positioning is out of scope. A shopper who picks the wrong aisle gets a correct route from the wrong place, and nothing in the system can detect it.

Substitutes are limited by what the catalogue records. FR-09 filters candidates on dietary attributes before the model sees them, so an unrecorded attribute reads as unknown and the candidate is excluded. That is the safe direction to fail, but it means thin catalogue data produces thin substitutes.

One store, one shopper. No concurrency, no live stock feed, no second store running at the same time. The portability test swaps the data files; it does not run two stores at once.
